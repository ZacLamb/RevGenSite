Website Design for Remodelers: Portfolios That Win the Bid

Homeowners planning a remodel spend weeks researching before they call anyone. Your website is where they decide whether you're the kind of company they trust with their kitchen and their savings.

The problem in carpentry and remodeling

Remodeling customers shop slowly and carefully. They browse portfolios on their phone at night, send links to their spouse, compare three or four companies and come back to the sites that made them feel confident. Many remodeling websites don't hold up to that kind of attention.

What we see most often:

  • The portfolio undersells the range. A grid of twenty kitchen photos hides the fact that you also do built-ins, staircases, additions and full-home renovations.
  • No explanation of how a project works. Homeowners worry about cost overruns, living through construction and how decisions get made. A site that doesn't address those fears leaves them with your competitor's site that does.
  • Every inquiry looks the same. "I'd like a quote" could be a $2,000 trim repair or a $150,000 addition, and you won't know until you call back.
  • Pages don't match searches. Someone searching for a bathroom remodeler in their town finds a homepage about "quality craftsmanship," with nothing about bathrooms or their town.
  • Old photos and no updates. The best work from the last two years never made it onto the site.

What we build for remodelers

  1. Service pages by project type. Kitchen remodeling, bathroom remodeling, basement finishing, additions, custom carpentry and built-ins, and whole-home renovation each get a page with matching photos, a description of typical scope and common questions.
  2. A portfolio organized by room and town. Project write-ups that tell a short story: what the homeowners wanted, what you changed, finish choices and the town. Each project can be linked from the relevant service and town pages.
  3. A "how we work" page. Your actual process, from first consult through design, selections, construction, client updates and final walkthrough, so prospects know what to expect before the first meeting.
  4. Town pages for your service area. Written around the homes and projects you see in each town, not a stock paragraph with a different town name.
  5. A consultation form that screens. Project type, rough size, budget range, timeline and photo uploads, routed into your CRM so your estimator can prepare.
  6. Trust details where they matter. Licensing and insurance information, warranty terms you actually offer, and real reviews placed next to the projects they describe.

How it works

  1. Discovery. We learn what projects you want more of, where you work and what makes your company different.
  2. Design. We design around your best photography and your brand, not a template with your logo on it.
  3. Build. We write and build every service, portfolio, process and town page.
  4. Launch and grow. We connect the site to your CRM and tracking, and keep adding projects and pages as the business grows.

What to look for (even if you don't hire us)

If you're interviewing web designers for a remodeling company, ask:

  • Will the portfolio be organized by project type, and can you add new projects easily?
  • Will project pages include written descriptions, or just photos?
  • Is there a page that explains your process from consult to walkthrough?
  • Does the contact form ask for project type, budget range and timeline, and send that into your CRM?
  • Will town pages be written individually?
  • Who writes the copy, and how much input do they need from you?
  • How fast will the portfolio load on a phone with dozens of large photos?

FAQ

Common questions

What should a remodeling company's portfolio include?

Organize it by project type, such as kitchens, bathrooms, basements, additions and finish carpentry, and give each project a short write-up: the starting problem, what changed, the town and roughly how long it took. Before-and-after pairs and a few detail shots of the carpentry do more than a large grid of unlabeled photos.

Should a remodeler's website ask about budget?

Yes, gently. A budget range dropdown on the consultation form, alongside project type and timeline, helps you prepare for the first call and spot mismatches early. Explain that it's to recommend the right scope, not to upsell.

Do remodelers need a page explaining their process?

It's one of the most useful pages you can have. A remodel is a big, unfamiliar purchase, and a plain walk-through of your steps, from consultation and design to selections, construction and walkthrough, answers the questions that otherwise stall a sale.
