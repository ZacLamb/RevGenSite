Landscaping Marketing Calendar: A Month-by-Month Plan for New England

October 18, 2026 · Digital Marketing

Landscaping in New England runs on a calendar the weather writes for you. The phones ring all at once in April, go quiet in July, spike again in October, and then it snows. Most landscapers market when they're slow and stop when they're busy, which is exactly backward. This calendar lays out what to do each month, both for marketing and for the operations that make the marketing pay off.

The big picture

A New England landscaping year has four selling seasons:

Period What you're selling Who you're selling to
January to March Maintenance contracts, spring cleanups, design/build Past customers first, then new leads
April to June Installs, upgrades, add-on services Current customers and new leads
July to September Fall cleanups, overseeding, hardscape projects Current customers, new design/build leads
October to December Snow contracts, next year's renewals Current customers and commercial accounts

The pattern is simple: sell each season a couple of months before it starts. By the time a homeowner thinks about spring cleanup, your best customers should already be booked.

January

Marketing:

  • Send maintenance contract renewals to every current customer. Make renewal easy with an online agreement and card on file.
  • Update your website with last season's best project photos.
  • Post a "year in review" on your Google Business Profile with photos.

Operations:

  • Snow operations, if you offer them.
  • Set prices for the coming season.
  • Review last year's numbers: which services made money, which customers were worth keeping.

February

Marketing:

  • Send an early spring cleanup booking offer to past customers.
  • Start design/build marketing. Homeowners plan patios, walls and plantings in winter.
  • Run search ads for design/build terms if you want more of that work.

Operations:

  • Book design consultations for spring installs.
  • Start hiring for the season. Good crew members get snapped up early.

March

Marketing:

  • Push spring cleanups to new leads through your website, Google Business Profile and local ads.
  • Follow up with every past customer who hasn't booked yet.
  • Make sure your website's spring cleanup page is current with real pricing guidance or a quote form.

Operations:

  • Equipment maintenance before the rush.
  • Map your routes. Clustering customers by town saves hours every week.

April

Marketing:

  • Keep cleanup and maintenance ads running only until your schedule is full, then shift budget to design/build.
  • Start asking for reviews from every completed cleanup.

Operations:

  • Spring cleanups, first mowings, mulch and edging.
  • Respond to every new lead fast. This is when slow response costs the most. See speed to lead for home services.

May

Marketing:

  • Offer add-ons to maintenance customers: mulch refresh, plantings, irrigation start-up.
  • Share before-and-after photos from spring installs on social media and your Google Business Profile.

Operations:

  • Full maintenance routes.
  • Design/build installs underway.

June

Marketing:

  • Keep collecting reviews and photos.
  • Promote irrigation, lighting or outdoor living upgrades if you offer them.

Operations:

  • Summer maintenance rhythm.
  • Track estimate follow-up closely. Design/build estimates from spring often stall here. An estimate follow-up sequence keeps them moving.

July

Marketing:

  • Start fall cleanup and overseeding pre-booking for current customers.
  • Promote hardscape projects for late summer and fall install.

Operations:

  • Heat and drought management for lawns.
  • Mid-season check-ins with maintenance customers.

August

Marketing:

  • Push overseeding and aeration. Late summer into early fall is generally the best window for cool-season grasses in New England.
  • Start fall cleanup booking for new leads.
  • Begin snow contract outreach to commercial accounts if you plow.

Operations:

  • Plan fall cleanup routes and crew capacity.

September

Marketing:

  • Fall cleanup ads and email to past customers who haven't booked.
  • Snow contract sales to residential customers.

Operations:

  • Overseeding and aeration.
  • Equipment prep for fall.

October

Marketing:

  • Fall cleanup bookings should be mostly closed out.
  • Close snow contracts.
  • Ask maintenance customers for reviews before the season ends.

Operations:

  • Fall cleanups and leaf removal.
  • Close out design/build projects before the ground freezes.

November

Marketing:

  • Holiday lighting or seasonal decorating if you offer it.
  • Thank-you message to all customers, with a renewal preview for next year.

Operations:

  • Final cleanups.
  • Fertilizer timing: Massachusetts regulations prohibit applying fertilizer to turf when the ground is frozen, snow-covered or the turf is dormant (state fact sheet), so late-season applications need to happen before that.
  • Equipment winterizing.

December

Marketing:

  • Early renewal offers for next season's maintenance contracts.
  • Share your best project of the year on your Google Business Profile and website.

Operations:

  • Snow operations.
  • Year-end review: revenue by service, customer retention, which marketing channels brought in the best jobs.

Adjust the calendar for your business mix

No two landscaping companies run the same mix, so shift the plan to fit yours:

  • Mostly maintenance: Renewals and spring cleanup booking are your whole year. Put most of your marketing effort into January through March, and spend summer collecting reviews and upselling add-ons.
  • Heavy design/build or hardscape: Your selling season starts earlier and runs longer. Design consultations booked in February become May and June installs, and late-summer leads can fill fall install slots before the ground freezes.
  • Snow services: Snow contracts compete for your attention with fall cleanups. Start commercial outreach in August so you aren't selling plowing in the middle of leaf season.
  • Coastal or seasonal markets: On Cape Cod and the islands, second-home owners often book from out of state. Email and online booking matter more than yard signs there.

Whatever the mix, keep ad budgets flexible. Pause spend when crews are booked out, and turn it back up when the schedule shows gaps.

Make the calendar run itself

The calendar only works if the messages go out on time, every year. That's hard to do from memory in the middle of spring cleanup season. A CRM can run most of it:

  • Tag customers by service. Maintenance, cleanup only, design/build, snow. Each tag gets the right campaigns.
  • Schedule recurring campaigns. Spring cleanup offers in February, fall cleanup in July, snow contracts in August, renewals in December. Set them once and adjust each year.
  • Automate review requests after every completed job.
  • Re-book automatically. When a spring cleanup is done, offer the fall cleanup. When fall is done, offer next spring.

For customers who haven't booked in a year or more, a reactivation text campaign is often the cheapest way to fill early-season gaps.

FAQ

Common questions

When should landscapers start marketing spring cleanups in New England?

Start in February. Past customers should get an early booking offer before the snow melts, so your spring schedule is mostly full before new leads start calling in March and April.

When should landscapers sell maintenance contracts?

Renew existing contracts in January and February, and sell new ones from February through April when homeowners are thinking about the season ahead.

What should a landscaping company do for marketing in winter?

Winter is for contract renewals, snow services if you offer them, design consultations for spring installs, review collection, and updating your website and Google Business Profile with last season's best work.
