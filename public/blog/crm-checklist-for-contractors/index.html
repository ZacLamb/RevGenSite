CRM Checklist for Contractors: What to Check Before You Buy

Updated October 18, 2026 · CRM & Systems

Most CRM software is designed for sales teams selling software, not for a five-person crew running service calls or a landscaper juggling weekly maintenance and one-off design jobs. That mismatch is why so many contractors buy a CRM, use it for three months and quietly go back to a spreadsheet. This checklist helps you pick one that fits how a trade business actually runs, and set it up so your team keeps using it.

Map your process before you look at software

Before any demo, write down what happens between "phone rings" and "invoice paid." Do it with whoever answers your phone, because they know the real version.

For most trades it looks something like this:

Stage What happens Who owns it
New inquiry Call, text, form or message comes in Office
Contacted Someone has reached the customer Office
Estimate scheduled Site visit is on the calendar Office / estimator
Estimate sent Price is in the customer's hands Estimator
Follow-up Waiting on a decision Office (or automation)
Booked Deposit taken or date confirmed Office
In progress Crew is on the job Crew lead
Complete Work done, invoice sent Office
Paid Money in, review requested Office (or automation)

A remodeler might add stages for design and material selection. An HVAC company might split installs from service calls and maintenance plans. A landscaper may need one pipeline for design/build and another for recurring maintenance. The point is that your stages come first. If a CRM can't be configured to match them, you'll fight it every day. We cover this in more depth in CRM pipeline stages for service businesses.

The checklist: what actually matters

Take this list into every demo and ask the salesperson to show you, not tell you.

Communication

  • Two-way texting from a local business number, with the full conversation saved on the contact
  • Calls, texts, emails, web forms and Facebook/Instagram messages landing in one shared inbox
  • Call recording or at least call logging on the contact record
  • A mobile app your techs and estimators will actually open

Pipeline and scheduling

  • Pipeline stages you can rename, add and remove yourself
  • Multiple pipelines if you run different kinds of work
  • A calendar that handles booking, reminders and reschedules without double entry
  • Online booking you can embed on your website

Automation

  • Triggers based on pipeline stage, tag, form submission or missed call
  • Follow-up sequences with a stop condition (the sequence ends when the customer replies or books)
  • Review requests tied to job completion
  • The ability to build and edit automations without paying a developer every time

Reporting

  • Lead source tracking that follows a lead to a booked job, not just a form fill
  • A view of open estimates and their age
  • Simple reports an owner can read on a phone

Practical stuff

  • Data export, so you can leave if you need to
  • Integrations with your accounting, payments and field service tools, or an open API
  • Clear pricing for users, phone numbers and texting usage
  • US texting compliance support, including A2P 10DLC registration (see our A2P 10DLC guide)

Questions to ask in the demo

Salespeople demo the polished parts. These questions get to the rest:

  1. "Show me how a missed call becomes a contact with a text sent back. How many clicks does it take to set up?"
  2. "If a customer replies to a follow-up text, what stops the sequence?"
  3. "Can I see which ad or website page a booked job came from?"
  4. "Who sets this up for me, and what does training include?"
  5. "What happens to my data if I cancel? What format is the export?"
  6. "What does texting cost per message, and is A2P registration included or extra?"
  7. "Can my techs use the app with spotty cell service?"

If an answer is "our partners can build that for you," find out who those partners are and what they charge before you sign.

Don't buy more than you'll use

Enterprise CRMs sell on feature lists. A five-person crew doesn't need forecasting dashboards or territory management. It needs the phone to stop ringing into a void and the estimate that went out Tuesday to get followed up on Thursday.

A useful test: list the five things you want the CRM to do in the first 30 days. If the platform does those five well, the other 200 features don't matter yet. If it needs a consultant to make those five work, keep looking or budget for the setup honestly.

Migration is where CRM projects fail

The software rarely kills a CRM project. The rollout does. Contacts get imported with duplicates, automations get built and never switched on, and the team drifts back to old habits because nobody owned the change. A short migration plan prevents most of that:

  1. Clean before you import. Merge duplicates, fix phone number formats, and tag past customers versus open leads.
  2. Pick one owner. One person is responsible for the CRM. Not a committee.
  3. Turn on automations one at a time. Test each with your own phone number before it goes live.
  4. Train by role. The office learns the inbox and pipeline. Techs learn the mobile app. The owner learns the reports.
  5. Set a cutover date. After that date, a lead that isn't in the CRM doesn't exist. Old spreadsheets go read-only.
  6. Review at 30 days. Look at what people are skipping and fix the setup, not the people.

Once the CRM is live, the next question is whether you can trust the lead source data. Our post on tracking where your leads come from covers call tracking and form attribution.

FAQ

Common questions

Do small contractors really need a CRM?

If leads come in by phone, text, web form and social messages, and more than one person touches them, yes. A CRM is the one place where every lead, estimate and follow-up lives, so nothing depends on someone's memory or a group text.

What is the difference between a CRM and field service software?

Field service tools focus on dispatch, work orders and invoicing. A CRM focuses on the path from inquiry to booked job: lead capture, follow-up, pipeline stages and marketing. Some shops use both, connected, and some platforms do a bit of each.

How long does a CRM migration take?

It depends on how messy your current data is and how many automations you need. Plan for a cleanup step before import, a short testing period, and training time for your team, rather than a same-day switch.
