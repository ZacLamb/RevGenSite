Glass Repair Insurance Claim Process for Glass Companies

November 25, 2026 · CRM & Systems

An insurance-paid glass job looks like a normal repair until the invoice comes back unpaid because a photo, a claim number or a calibration report was missing. The glass work is the easy part. The money is lost in intake, documentation and follow-up.

This post lays out a workflow you can run with a spreadsheet, a clipboard or a CRM: what to ask on the first call, which photos to take, how to handle authorization and billing, and how to track every claim until it is paid.

Know who owes you what

Most insurance glass jobs have more than one payer, and mixing them up causes most billing headaches. Sort it out at intake.

Party Typically pays What you need from them
Customer Deductible, plus anything not covered Signed estimate, payment method, consent to bill the insurer
Insurer or its claims administrator The covered amount Claim number, authorization, completed invoice and documentation
Property owner or manager (commercial jobs) Deductible, or the full job if the claim is denied Authorization to proceed, billing contact, any PO number

Say your windshield job is $600 and the customer's glass deductible is $100. The customer owes you $100 and the insurer owes $500. If the insurer later reduces a line item, you need to know ahead of time who covers the difference. That conversation belongs at intake, not after the install.

As of 2026, deductibles for auto glass depend on the policy. Sources disagree on how Massachusetts law treats this. One industry summary says Massachusetts requires insurers to offer a $100 deductible option for glass damage under comprehensive coverage. That is an option the customer chooses, not an automatic waiver. Ask every customer what their deductible is and don't assume it is zero.

Intake: the questions to ask on the first call

Whoever answers the phone, including after hours, should ask the same questions in the same order. Put them on a card by the phone or in a form your team fills out.

  1. Is this going through insurance? If the customer is unsure, ask whether they have comprehensive coverage and whether they have called their carrier.
  2. Insurer, policy number and claim number if they have one.
  3. Deductible amount, as the customer understands it. Confirm it with the insurer when you verify the claim.
  4. Vehicle: year, make, model, VIN, and features such as a rain sensor, a camera behind the mirror, or a heated windshield. Property: address, glass type, size and location.
  5. Date and cause of damage. A rock chip, a break-in and a storm are three different claim types.
  6. Best contact method and name of the person who authorizes work, which matters for fleets and property managers.
  7. Consent to text and email for photo requests and appointment updates.

If you handle emergencies, see our post on after-hours emergency glass leads so the intake still happens at 9 p.m. When the damage is not a vehicle, you can often start with photos sent by the customer, as we cover in photo estimates for glass repair.

Photos that protect your invoice

Insurers pay for what the file supports. Photos are cheap, so take more than you think you need. Build a fixed set for each job type so no one has to remember.

Before work (auto glass):

  • Full vehicle, including the plate or VIN plate
  • Close-up of the damage with a coin or ruler for scale
  • The full windshield from inside and outside
  • Any existing damage, including cracks, dings and trim problems, so you aren't blamed for it later
  • Dashboard warning lights, if any are on

After work:

  • Finished glass and trim from outside and inside
  • The camera or sensor bracket area, if one exists
  • Dashboard with no warning lights showing

Property glass (storefronts, windows, doors, shower enclosures):

  • Wide shot showing the opening and surroundings
  • Close-up of breakage, plus any temporary board-up
  • Tempering stamp, label or etching on the glass, if visible
  • Measurements with a tape visible in the photo

Name the files with the claim number or the job ID. Store them on the job record, not in a technician's phone gallery.

Authorization and calibration paperwork

Don't start until you have authorization or the customer has agreed to pay if the claim falls through. Write down who authorized the work, when, and the approved amount. If the final price changes, get the change approved before you bill, not after.

Calibration is where invoices are most often questioned. As of 2026, expect to document why it was needed and that it was done. One shop-software vendor says insurers want proof that the manufacturer's procedure requires the calibration, and that expectations vary by insurer and policy. See their guide to ADAS calibration reimbursement. A practical packet includes:

  • The pre-scan and post-scan results
  • The manufacturer procedure or position statement that applies
  • A dated calibration report tied to the VIN
  • Photos of the setup and the completed result

Some insurers want this packet before they pay, and others only if they question the line item. Gather it every time so you aren't scrambling.

A billing workflow with clear stages

A claim should always be in exactly one stage, with one person responsible for moving it forward. Here is a simple version, and our post on CRM pipeline stages explains how to design stages for your own shop.

  1. Intake complete. All seven intake items are filled in.
  2. Claim verified. You've confirmed coverage and deductible with the insurer or administrator.
  3. Scheduled. Appointment set, parts ordered, customer notified.
  4. Work done. Before and after photos uploaded, calibration packet attached if needed.
  5. Invoice submitted. Sent with the claim number and documentation, with the date logged.
  6. Awaiting payment. A follow-up task fires at a set interval, say 14 days.
  7. Paid or disputed. If disputed, record why and who you contacted.
  8. Closed. Deductible collected, review request sent.

Collect the deductible on completion. Unpaid deductibles are small, but they add up and they are awkward to chase weeks later.

Know your local rules on shop choice

Don't rely on what a customer or an insurer rep tells you about whether you're allowed to do the job. Read the statute. Massachusetts General Laws chapter 175, section 113X says that an insurer may not require a person to use a particular registered motor vehicle glass repair shop. It also says that once a shop is chosen, the insurer or third-party biller can't send the work or the customer's contact information to a different shop without the insured's consent. The same section allows insurers to provide information to help the insured select or schedule a shop.

The statute refers to registered motor vehicle glass repair shops as provided in chapter 100A, so confirm your own registration is current. This is general information, not legal advice. If a claims administrator is pressuring your customer or refusing to deal with you, talk to a lawyer or the Massachusetts Division of Insurance. If you work in other states, check their rules separately because they differ.

Set it up so nothing depends on memory

You can run this process on paper. The problem comes when the owner is on a ladder, the office manager is out and a claim sits at "invoice submitted" for six weeks. A CRM can handle the repetitive parts:

  • A required-fields intake form, so a job can't move forward without a claim number
  • A photo checklist on each job record
  • Automatic tasks for payment follow-up
  • A report of every claim older than 30 days and unpaid
  • Texts to the customer for photo requests and appointment reminders, sent only where you have consent. If you text, see our A2P 10DLC registration guide.

FAQ

Common questions

What information do I need from a customer to start an insurance glass job?

Get the insurer name, policy and claim numbers, the vehicle VIN or the property address, the date and cause of the damage, and the customer's deductible. If you skip any of these at intake, you will chase them later when the invoice is already waiting.

Does insurance usually pay for ADAS calibration after a windshield replacement?

When calibration is part of a covered replacement, it is typically included, but coverage and paperwork requirements vary by insurer and policy. Plan to document why the calibration was needed and that it was completed, and tell the customer to confirm with their carrier.

Do I have to be an insurer's preferred shop to do a glass claim?

It depends on your state and the insurer's rules, so check before you promise anything to a customer. In Massachusetts, as of 2026, the statute limits how insurers can steer customers to a particular registered glass shop. Read it before you build your intake script.
