Shower Door Installer Website: What Homeowners Check First

October 27, 2026 · Web Design & SEO

A homeowner looking at a new shower door is about to spend real money on something they'll see every day. Before they call, they'll scroll through three or four glass companies' sites and quietly rank them. This post covers what they check, so your site passes the test.

What homeowners do before they call

Most people start with a search like "shower door installer near me" or "frameless shower enclosure [town]." They open a few results on their phone, often while standing in the bathroom looking at the tub.

They're trying to answer four questions fast:

  1. Does this company install the kind of door I'm picturing?
  2. Does their work look good in a bathroom like mine?
  3. Roughly what is this going to cost, and what happens next?
  4. Can I trust them in my home?

If your site doesn't answer those within a few scrolls, they back out and call the next company. That holds even if your installs are better.

Show real installs, sorted by job type

Photos do most of the selling in this trade. Glass looks different in every bathroom because of the tile, lighting, hardware and layout.

A few rules that work:

  • Use your own photos. Stock images of perfect spa bathrooms make your site look like every other one. Google's own guidance for Business Profile photos asks for real images of your services, and the same logic applies to your website.
  • Sort by job type. Make separate galleries or sections for frameless swing doors, sliding/bypass doors, inline panels, neo-angle corners, steam enclosures and tub enclosures. A homeowner who wants a steam shower doesn't want to dig through 80 tub-door photos.
  • Add a one-line caption to each photo. For example: "Frameless 3/8" clear glass, matte black hardware, niche wall." Those details help homeowners picture their own job.
  • Show the whole bathroom, then a close-up of the hardware. Hinges, handles and header details are where people judge quality.
  • Photograph at the end of the job. Take a few shots before you pack the truck. Clean glass, good light, no tools in frame.

If you want to see how this ties into your Google listing, the Google Business Profile checklist for contractors covers photo uploads there. Note that photos and videos show up on Google only after you verify your Business Profile, per Google's help page.

Explain glass options in plain English

Homeowners don't know the difference between framed and frameless, or why one costs more than another. If your site doesn't explain it, they'll guess, or they'll find a competitor's page that does.

Build one clear options page, and link to it from every service page. A simple table works well:

Option What to explain on your site
Frameless, semi-frameless, framed Look, how much metal shows, and how each is typically priced relative to the others
Glass thickness Which thicknesses you install and where each is used
Clear vs. low-iron vs. frosted or textured Color, privacy, and how each looks against tile
Hardware finishes Chrome, brushed nickel, matte black, brass: show real photos of each
Door style Swing, sliding, fixed panel, corner, steam
Protective coating What you offer, and what cleaning still looks like

Also explain the part people find surprising: tempered glass can't be cut or drilled after it's tempered. So a custom door is measured first, then fabricated. That's why you measure carefully and why there's a wait between measure and install. Say it once, simply, and homeowners understand why you ask questions.

If you also do custom mirrors, glass railings, tabletops or window work, give each its own page. These are different searches and different buyers. The local service pages post explains why separate pages for separate jobs tend to work better than one catch-all page.

Be honest about price and process

Almost nobody expects an exact number from a website. They do want to know whether they're in the right ballpark and what the steps are.

You can do this without posting a price list:

  • List the factors that move the price: door style, glass thickness, hardware finish, size, and whether walls and curbs are out of plumb.
  • Say what you need to quote: photos, rough dimensions, or an in-home measure.
  • State your real timeline for each step, such as quote, measure, fabrication and install. If you don't know your usual range, work it out before writing it down.
  • Explain what happens if the measurements show a problem on site.

A simple "send us photos" path helps a lot. Our post on photo estimates for glass repair walks through how that works, and instant quote calculators cover the option of giving a rough range online. Say your average shower door job is $1,800: if a form gets you a decent photo and a phone number, you can often tell the homeowner whether it's a fit before you drive anywhere.

Show the trust details homeowners look for

Glass goes in a place where people are barefoot and wet, so safety and workmanship matter to them. A few things to put in plain sight:

  • Your registration number, if it applies. Massachusetts has a Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) program for work on owner-occupied one-to-four-unit homes. Residential contractors who solicit, bid on, or perform work on such properties must be registered. Any website or advertising must display the HIC registration number, according to Mass.gov's consumer guidance and its contractor resources page. Whether a particular glass job is covered is a question for the state, not for this blog post. If you're unsure, ask them. As of 2026, that's what the pages say, and rules can change.
  • Insurance. Mass.gov's consumer tips tell homeowners to ask for proof of insurance, so expect to be asked. A short line on your site about coverage saves a phone call.
  • Reviews with detail. A review that says "they measured twice and the door fits perfectly" is worth more than a row of stars. See how to turn Google reviews into marketing for ways to use them on your site.
  • Your real service area. List the towns you actually serve, and make the page specific rather than a long list of names.
  • Who shows up. A photo of your crew and a sentence about who measures and who installs.

Make it easy to reach you on a phone

Most visitors will be on a phone, and many will be in the bathroom. A few basics:

  • A tap-to-call button that stays on screen as they scroll.
  • A short quote form: name, phone, town, door type, and a photo upload. Skip anything else.
  • Pages that load fast. Big gallery photos are the usual culprit, so resize them before uploading. Our post on website speed and local rankings covers the basics.
  • Easy-to-read text with no pop-ups covering the content.
  • A way to reach you after hours, even if it's just a form that promises a next-morning callback.

A quick checklist to audit your site

Open your site on your phone and check each one:

  1. Is the first screen a photo of your own work with a phone button?
  2. Can a visitor find the door type they want in two taps?
  3. Does every photo have a caption with glass type, hardware and style?
  4. Is there an options page that explains frameless, thickness and finishes in plain language?
  5. Is the quote process explained, including what you need from them?
  6. Is your registration number (if required) visible on the pages and in the footer?
  7. Are reviews on the site, and do they mention specifics?
  8. Does the quote form work on a phone and send you a notification?
  9. Do your town and service pages say something different from each other?

If you can't answer yes to most of these, fix those first. They cost less than ads and they make every ad dollar work harder.

FAQ

Common questions

What should a shower door installer's website show?

Lead with real photos of your finished installs, sorted by type (frameless, sliding, steam, tub enclosures). Add plain-language glass and hardware options, a clear quote process, your service area, and a phone number that works with one tap.

Should I put prices on my shower door website?

You don't have to post exact prices, but a ranges-and-factors explanation helps. Say what moves the price (glass thickness, hardware finish, door style, wall conditions) and how quickly you can give a firm quote.

Do I need to show a license number on my glass company website in Massachusetts?

If your residential work falls under the state's Home Improvement Contractor program, Massachusetts requires your registration number in your advertising, and that includes your website. Check Mass.gov or ask the state whether your jobs are covered.
