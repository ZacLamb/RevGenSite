Chimney Repair Marketing: Get Found Before Freeze-Thaw Hits

October 26, 2026 · Digital Marketing

Most homeowners don't notice chimney damage until water stains show up on a ceiling or a brick lands in the yard. By then the freeze-thaw cycle has been working for a while, and the homeowner is searching in a hurry. If your marketing only starts when they do, you're competing with every other contractor who answers the phone.

This guide covers how a masonry company can set up chimney repair marketing so you show up early, look credible, and turn searches into booked estimates.

Why timing drives chimney repair marketing

Bricks and mortar absorb water. When temperatures drop, that water freezes and expands, and repeated cycles break down the brick face. Chimneys take it harder than most masonry, because they stay colder and wetter than walls at ground level.

The tricky part for marketing is that freeze-thaw damage in mortar joints often starts on the inside of the joint and only shows on the outside years later. Homeowners rarely see it coming. Many contractors see the fallout in spring, since winter damage is commonly found during spring inspections.

So you have two windows. In fall, you sell prevention to people who have a hunch. In spring, you sell repair to people who just found out. Here's a rough calendar you can adapt to your own crew schedule:

Window Homeowner mindset What to publish or run
Sept–Nov "Should I get this checked before winter?" Pre-winter inspection pages, crown and repointing photos, search ads for repair terms
Dec–Feb "There's a leak near the chimney" Updated profile hours, fast call handling, estimate follow-up
Mar–May "What did winter do to it?" Spring damage posts, before/after galleries, review requests
Summer "Let's get it done right" Larger rebuild and restoration work, referral asks

Sell the problems homeowners search for

Nobody types "tuckpointing." They type "chimney leaking," "crumbling mortar," "bricks falling off chimney" or "chimney crown cracked." Build your pages and profile services around those phrases and the work behind them.

One page per job type works well:

  • Chimney repointing: mortar joints cut out and refilled
  • Chimney crown repair or replacement: the cap at the top that sheds water
  • Spalling brick replacement: flaking or crumbling brick faces
  • Partial or full chimney rebuild: above the roofline or down to the firebox
  • Water intrusion diagnosis: leaks showing up in the attic or on a ceiling

On each page, show homeowners the warning signs. Useful ones include flaking brick, white powdery stains called efflorescence, cracked or missing mortar and stains on ceilings or attic walls near the chimney. A page that helps someone diagnose their own chimney earns trust before they call.

Use your expertise as content. Mortar choice is a good example. Hard, cement-rich mortar on soft brick can push stress into the brick instead of the joint, and old mortar generally needs to be removed to roughly three-quarters of an inch to give the new mortar something to grip. Explaining that in plain language tells a homeowner you know what you're doing, and it separates you from the guy with a hardware-store bag of mix.

Set up your Google Business Profile for chimney work

Your profile is usually the first thing a homeowner sees. A few settings matter most. If you want the full list, see our Google Business Profile checklist for contractors.

  1. Pick the right primary category. Both "Masonry contractor" and "Chimney services" are categories on Google's list, and so is "Chimney sweep." Google's guidance, as summarized by one category directory, is to choose the most specific category that describes what your business is, and to use as few as possible. As of 2026 you can add up to nine additional categories, but only add ones you really perform.
  2. Hide your address if customers don't visit you. Google's own guidelines say that a service-area business should hide its address from customers. Then list the towns you actually work in. One current guide notes you can add up to 20 cities or postal codes within about two hours' drive of your base.
  3. List your services by name. Add repointing, crown repair, brick replacement and chimney rebuilds as separate services, with a one-line plain description of each.
  4. Post real photos. Close-ups of failed joints, a cracked crown, a finished repoint. Tag them to the town if you can do so honestly.
  5. Put your HIC number where the state expects it. Massachusetts says building permits, contracts and advertising for residential contracting must carry the contractor's active registration number. The state also tells homeowners to look for the number on a contractor's website or advertising. Details are on the Mass.gov HIC page. Larger chimney work may also involve a Construction Supervisor License, and the Massachusetts Chimney Sweep Guild lists both the HIC registration and the CSL among the credentials homeowners should verify. Confirm with the state what applies to your jobs.

Collect reviews the way Google allows

Chimney work is a trust purchase. Someone is letting a crew work on their roof. Reviews that mention the specific job (a repoint, a crown replacement) help the next homeowner picture the work.

As of 2026, the safe rules are simple:

  • Ask every customer, not just the happy ones. Pre-screening by sentiment is called review gating, and it is against Google's rules.
  • Offer nothing in return. Google prohibits incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services in exchange for posting, changing or removing a review.
  • Don't script the review. Ask customers to describe the work in their own words.
  • Send the link by text after the job. Some 2026 policy summaries also flag review kiosks and pressuring customers on your premises, so a follow-up text avoids the issue.

Our post on turning Google reviews into marketing covers what to do with them once you have them.

Decide whether paid ads fit

Search ads can catch the fall rush and the spring scramble, because they run exactly when you want them to. Aim them at repair-intent searches and at the towns you really serve, and send clicks to the matching service page instead of your home page.

Google Local Services Ads are worth checking, but eligibility isn't universal. As of 2026, eligibility is set per country, category and location. The category lists I found mention trades like roofing and tree service, and I didn't see masonry or chimney repair clearly listed. Use Google's eligibility checker for your ZIP code before planning around it. Our comparison of Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads explains the trade-offs.

If you're also weighing a bigger site rebuild, see what a mason's website needs and our project gallery guide for hardscaping and masonry.

Don't lose the lead after the click

A chimney job is often booked from a rooftop or a ladder, which means missed calls. Set up a missed call text-back so the homeowner gets a reply within a minute. Then follow up on estimates with a short, scheduled sequence (estimate follow-up for contractors walks through one).

Finally, track where each lead came from. Even a simple "How did you find us?" field on every estimate will tell you whether fall prevention pages, spring ads or the Google profile are actually paying off.

FAQ

Common questions

When is the best time to market chimney repair and repointing?

Start in early fall, before the first hard freezes, and run a second push in early spring when winter damage becomes visible. Keep your Google profile photos, reviews and service pages current all winter so you are found when homeowners finally notice a problem.

Should my primary Google category be Masonry contractor or Chimney services?

Pick the one that best describes the work you most want. Both categories exist, and Google asks for the most specific primary category with as few extras as possible. A full-service mason usually leads with Masonry contractor and adds Chimney services only if chimney work is a real service line.

Do I have to show my HIC number in my chimney repair ads in Massachusetts?

Massachusetts says advertising for residential contracting must carry the contractor's active HIC registration number, and the state tells homeowners to look for it on websites and ads. Put it in your site footer, ads, estimates and truck lettering, and check with the state if you are unsure what applies to your jobs.
