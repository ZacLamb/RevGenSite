Masonry Contractor Waitlist: Manage Backlog, Keep Leads Warm

November 7, 2026 · CRM & Systems

Good masons are booked out. That's a nice problem until the people on your list quietly hire someone else because you went silent for six weeks. A masonry contractor waitlist only works if it's organized, honest about timing, and kept warm without a lot of daily effort.

Here's how to run one, whether you use a notebook, a spreadsheet or a CRM.

A waitlist is a pipeline, not a notebook

Most masonry waitlists live in a mix of texts, a clipboard in the truck, and the owner's memory. The problem isn't the people on the list. It's that nobody can answer simple questions: Who's next? Who still wants the job? Who was promised a June start?

Treat the waitlist as a set of stages with an owner and a date for each lead. If you want a deeper look at designing stages, read our post on CRM pipeline stages for a service business. For masonry, the key shift is this: the waitlist isn't one list. It's several lists sorted by the kind of work, because different jobs wait differently.

Sort the backlog by job type

A leaning chimney and a patio for next summer should never share a queue. Here's a simple way to sort the backlog:

Job type Example How it waits What to do
Safety or water damage Loose chimney crown, failing steps, leaking veneer Poorly Triage first, offer a quick site visit or a temporary fix recommendation
Repair work Repointing, small wall repairs Okay, if you set a window Slot into open days between big jobs
Planned installs Patios, walkways, retaining walls Well, but quotes can go stale Confirm scope and a seasonal start window
Large projects Full veneer, outdoor kitchens, multi-wall jobs Very well Plan materials and crew in advance, update often

If you do chimney or repointing work, our posts on chimney repair marketing for masons and tuckpointing and repointing marketing cover how those leads tend to come in and why speed matters on the urgent ones.

Weather matters too. Masonry in New England is seasonal, and mortar work generally gets harder as temperatures drop. Say your spring schedule fills in February. By then, the right question for a new lead isn't "Can you fit me in?" It's "Which window fits this job?"

Give every lead an honest start window

"Soon" is what makes people leave. A date range makes them stay. Before a lead goes on the list, get these answers and write them down:

  • What exactly is the job? Photos help, and so does a site visit for anything large.
  • How flexible is the timing? Some homeowners can wait for fall. Others have a closing, a party or a leak.
  • What's the real deadline, if any? A graduation party in June is a different lead than "sometime this year."
  • Who decides? A couple, a landlord, a property manager.
  • What's the best way to reach them? Text, call or email, and whether it's okay to text.

Then quote a window, not a day. "Late May to mid June" is believable. A specific date you may need to move is a promise you'll break.

Build waitlist stages you can run in a CRM

Here's a stage setup that works for a busy mason. Rename the stages to fit your shop:

  1. New inquiry: Contact made, details not collected yet.
  2. Site visit scheduled: A time is booked.
  3. Estimate sent: Quote delivered with an expiration date.
  4. Waitlisted, window set: Accepted or interested, with a target month.
  5. Materials and scope confirmed: Selections made, anything special-order identified.
  6. Contract signed, date scheduled: On the calendar.
  7. Released or lost: Out of the queue, with a reason recorded.

The last stage matters more than it looks. If you record why people leave (price, timing, found someone else), you learn whether your backlog is a timing problem or a pricing problem. For more on tying leads back to sources, see how to track where your leads come from.

Also give each stage a rule. For example: nobody sits in "Estimate sent" for more than a week without a follow-up. Our post on an estimate follow-up sequence for contractors has a ready-to-use cadence.

Keep leads warm without nagging

People on a waitlist don't want a sales pitch. They want to know they haven't been forgotten. A good touch tells them something real.

Here's a simple cadence for a lead waiting a few months:

  • Day of estimate: Thank-you and a recap of scope and start window.
  • Two to three weeks later: A quick check on whether the timing still works.
  • Monthly: A schedule update, such as "We're finishing a wall in town and expect to start your area in May."
  • Two to three weeks before start: Confirm scope, access, parking, and material selections.

Each message should do a job: confirm timing, ask a question, or give a real update. A photo of a recently finished project is better than a generic "just checking in." If you have a strong gallery, a project gallery on your website gives waiting leads something to look at between messages, which helps them stay confident in their choice.

Speed still matters at the front of the funnel. The best waitlist is useless if the first call goes unanswered, so review speed to lead for home services too.

Texting rules to check (as of 2026)

Texting is the easiest way to keep a waitlist warm, and the rules are easy to get wrong. A few things worth knowing as of 2026:

  • Carrier registration: Business texts sent from a standard 10-digit number through software need A2P 10DLC registration. Since February 2025, carriers block SMS traffic from unregistered 10DLC numbers outright. See A2P 10DLC registration for small businesses for the steps, and a carrier-registration overview from Infobip for background.
  • Consent: Marketing or promotional texts generally require prior express written consent, which an electronic checkbox can satisfy. Capture it on your estimate form or intake page, and save the record. A TCPA guide from Infobip explains the basics.
  • Opt-outs: The FCC unanimously approved revised consent-revocation rules on September 30, 2026. One summary says that from November 25, 2026, an opt-out from a marketing text still reaches all of that sender's marketing. Details are in Kelley Drye's write-up and this FCC order summary. Whatever the final text says, honor every "stop" right away.

I'm not a lawyer, and this is general information. Have an attorney review your consent language and texting setup.

Be careful with deposits to hold a spot

It's tempting to ask for money to lock in a place on the schedule. In Massachusetts, home improvement work is covered by the Home Improvement Contractor law, and any deposit required before work starts can't exceed one-third of the total contract price, or the cost of special-order or custom materials that must be ordered in advance. The state explains this on its home improvement contract requirements page.

The law also expects a written contract with start and substantial completion dates. If you're promising a window, say so in the contract and keep your records straight. If a custom stone order drives the deposit, document what was ordered and why. Ask your attorney whether a separate "reservation" fee fits your situation before adding one.

When the backlog gets too long

A long list isn't automatically good news. If the wait stretches past what people will tolerate, you start losing leads who would have booked earlier. Options to consider:

  • Pause new estimates for large jobs and tell people when you'll reopen.
  • Offer a firm later window instead of an open-ended maybe.
  • Refer small jobs out to a mason you trust, so you keep goodwill.
  • Refresh old quotes before they go stale, since material and labor costs can change.

Here's a hypothetical to show why it matters. Say 40 people are on your list and your average job is $6,000. If 10 of them drift away because they never heard from you, that's $60,000 in work you'd have booked. A few minutes of consistent follow-up is cheap by comparison. FAQ

Common questions

How long should a masonry contractor keep someone on a waitlist?

Keep them on as long as you can give a believable start window. If your honest answer is more than a season out, tell them so and let them decide, rather than parking them with no date. Re-confirm interest every few weeks so the list reflects people who still want the work.

Can I charge a deposit to hold a spot on my masonry schedule in Massachusetts?

Massachusetts caps any deposit due before work starts on covered home improvement contracts at the greater of one-third of the contract price or the cost of special-order materials. Whether a separate hold fee fits within that is a question for your attorney, so get that answered before you add one to your process.

How often should I follow up with leads who are waiting on a masonry job?

A light touch every two to four weeks is a reasonable starting point, with each message carrying something useful such as a schedule update or a confirmation of their window. Stop or slow down for anyone who asks, and always honor opt-outs.
