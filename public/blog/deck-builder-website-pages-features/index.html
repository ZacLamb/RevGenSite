Deck Builder Website: The Pages and Features That Win Projects

November 5, 2026 · Web Design & SEO

Most deck builders have a website that is a logo, a gallery of nice photos, and a contact form. That gets you looked at, but it doesn't get you hired. A homeowner spending five figures on a deck wants to know what it will cost, whether you'll pull the permit, and whether you'll show up.

Here are the pages and features that answer those questions before the homeowner calls your competitor.

How homeowners shop for a deck

A deck is a planned purchase, not an emergency. People look at your site, then two or three others, then ask a neighbor. They often start the search in winter or early spring for a summer build, so your site is working long before your crew is.

That changes what the site has to do. It has to be easy to compare, easy to trust, and easy to act on from a phone. A homeowner standing in the backyard with a tape measure is a very common visitor.

Say your site gets 400 visitors a month. At a 1% estimate-request rate that's 4 requests. At 3% it's 12. If you close one in four and the average deck is $18,000, that's roughly $18,000 versus $54,000 in booked work from the same traffic. These numbers are hypothetical, but the math is why conversion matters as much as traffic.

The pages a deck builder website needs

Build one page per thing you want to be hired for. A single "Services" page with a bulleted list won't rank for much and doesn't persuade anyone.

Page What it answers What to include
Home Who are you and where do you work? Phone number, towns served, 3 to 5 best projects, estimate button
New deck construction What does a build with you look like? Sizes, shapes, stairs, lighting, timeline range, project photos
Deck replacement Can you tear out and rebuild my old deck? How you assess footings and framing, what gets reused
Repair and resurfacing Can my deck be saved? Signs of rot, loose ledgers and railings, when resurfacing makes sense
Materials PT, cedar, composite, PVC, or hardwood? Upkeep, lifespan, look, and which you recommend
Railings, pergolas, screened porches Can you do the add-ons? One section or page each, with photos
Gallery Is your work good? Individual project pages (see below)
Process What happens after I call? Visit, design, permit, build, final inspection
Service areas Do you work in my town? One page per town with local projects
Reviews Do your customers like you? Real reviews with first name and town
Estimate request How do I start? Short form with photo upload

If you're deciding what to build first, start with new construction, replacement, and your three biggest towns. You can add the rest over time.

Make the gallery do the selling

A wall of thumbnails doesn't help. Each project should be its own page with a few lines of detail. Our post on project gallery websites for hardscaping and masonry covers the same idea for stone and patio work, and it applies directly to decks.

For each project, include:

  • Town (not the street address) and the type of home
  • Size, height off the ground, and materials
  • The problem: an old deck that was rotting, a steep yard, a bad ledger
  • What you did and anything tricky about the build
  • 5 to 10 photos, including a before shot and a close-up of railings and stair details

Those details are what homeowners search for and what separates you from a builder with a stock-photo site. They also give you local content for every town you serve.

Answer the cost, permit, and code questions

Homeowners will ask about price and permits in the first conversation. Put the answer on the site and you'll spend less time on calls with people who were never going to hire you.

Cost. Write a page on what drives deck pricing. A good model is this Massachusetts retaining wall pricing page example, which shows how to discuss cost without locking yourself into a number. For decks, list size, height, material, railing type, stairs, lighting, site access, and permit work.

Permits. In Massachusetts, most decks need a permit, particularly if attached to the house, higher than 30 inches, or larger than 200 square feet, and requirements and fees vary by town. Ground-level, freestanding decks under 200 square feet are generally exempt, but local zoning setbacks still apply. Those summaries come from third-party guides (South Shore Decks and PermitsGuide), so tell homeowners to confirm with their town building department. Say plainly on your site who pulls the permit and what it adds to the job.

Footings. A short explainer on frost depth shows homeowners you build to last. Footings need to go below the frost line, commonly cited as roughly 42 to 48 inches deep depending on the town. Check your local requirement before you publish a number.

Show the credentials homeowners check

In Massachusetts, this is also a legal item. As of 2026, building permits, contracts, and advertising for residential contracting must contain the contractor's active registration number. Publishing a home improvement ad without the registration number is listed among prohibited practices. Details are on the Mass.gov HIC contractor page.

Put your HIC number in the footer of every page, and on your estimate page and any ads you run. It also helps you. The state tells homeowners to ask for the number and look up registration status and disciplinary history at ContractorHub.mass.gov (Mass.gov consumer guidance). If your number is already on the page, you look like a builder who expects to be checked.

A Construction Supervisor License (CSL) is a separate credential. An HIC registration is different from a CSL, and the two are not interchangeable. If you hold both, say so. Rules differ in other New England states, so confirm what applies where you work.

Other trust items worth adding:

  • Proof of insurance, described in plain words
  • Real reviews with first names and towns
  • Photos of your crew and trucks
  • A written warranty, if you offer one (state exactly what it covers)

Features that turn visits into estimate requests

Click-to-call on mobile. The phone number should be tappable and visible without scrolling on every page.

A short estimate form with a photo upload. Ask for name, phone, town, and project type. Then add optional fields: rough size, height off the ground, attached or freestanding, material preference, and timeline. Photos of the existing deck or yard save you a trip for projects that don't fit. If you want to go further, see instant quote calculators for your website.

Fast loading. Deck sites are image-heavy, and that's where they slow down. Google's Core Web Vitals report rates a page "good" at an LCP of 2.5 seconds or less, INP of 200 milliseconds or less, and CLS of 0.1 or less. Compress photos, size them to the layout, and don't autoplay big videos on the home page. More in our post on website speed and local rankings.

Lead tracking. Know which page and which source each estimate request came from. If you don't, you'll keep paying for things that don't work. See track where your leads come from.

A fast follow-up. The form is only half the job. If a request sits for two days in March, that homeowner has already talked to someone else. Read speed to lead for home services and estimate follow-up sequences for contractors.

A 10-minute audit of your own site

  1. Open your site on your phone. Can you tap to call in one move?
  2. Search "deck builder [your town]" and see whether you have a page for that town.
  3. Count your service pages. Is there one for replacements and one for repairs?
  4. Click into a gallery photo. Is there a project page with details?
  5. Look for your HIC number in the footer.
  6. Submit your own estimate form. How long until you get the notification?
  7. Run the home page through Google's PageSpeed Insights.
  8. Look for a page that explains cost and permits.

If you fail three or more, the site is probably losing you jobs. If you're shopping for help, our guide to hiring a web designer for your trade business lists the questions to ask.

FAQ

Common questions

What pages does a deck builder website need?

At minimum: a home page, one page for each deck type you want to build (new decks, replacements, repairs and resurfacing, railings, pergolas, screened porches), a materials page, a gallery of individual projects, service area pages, a process page, reviews, and an estimate request page. Each page should have a phone number and a way to request an estimate.

Should a deck builder put prices on their website?

You don't need exact prices, but a page that explains what drives cost (size, height, materials, railings, stairs, permits, site access) filters out poor-fit leads and builds trust. Ranges with clear caveats work better than silence, and a hypothetical example is better than nothing.

Do I have to show my contractor registration number on my website?

In Massachusetts, home improvement advertising generally has to carry your HIC registration number, and a website counts as advertising. Check the current rules on mass.gov, and check your own state's rules if you work elsewhere.
