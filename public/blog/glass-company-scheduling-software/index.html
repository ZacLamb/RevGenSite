Glass Company Scheduling Software: Measure, Order, Install

November 19, 2026 · Custom Tools

Most double trips in a glass shop are not install problems. They start at the measure visit or the order, and nobody catches them until a crew is standing in a bathroom with the wrong panel. This post covers the scheduling rules, job stages and software questions that keep a job to one measure trip and one install trip.

Where double trips come from

Glass is different from most trade work because custom pieces are made to order and can't be fixed on site. For tempered glass, it cannot be cut, scored, drilled or resized after it has been tempered (source). Notches and hardware cutouts also have to be specified before the panel is tempered. Once the order is placed, a mistake means a remake and a new lead time.

A second trip usually traces back to one of these:

  • The measure was off by a fraction, or taken before the framing, tile or drywall was finished.
  • Hinge, handle or hardware cutouts were not written down on the order.
  • The glass type was wrong for the location (more on that below).
  • The install was booked for a date the glass never made.
  • The installer showed up without the lift, extra hands or suction cups the job needed.
  • Nobody confirmed that the opening was clear, the power was on or the customer would be home.

Say your two-person crew costs you $120 an hour all in, and a wasted trip is three hours of loading, driving and unloading. That's $360 gone, before you count the customer's patience. Two of those a week adds up quickly.

Give every job a status that means something

Scheduling software only works if the statuses reflect real events, not opinions. Here is a simple set for a residential or commercial glass job. Adjust the names to fit your shop.

  1. Lead / estimate requested. Photos and rough dimensions on file.
  2. Measure scheduled. A date, a person and a list of what to capture.
  3. Measured, pending approval. Dimensions, glass type, hardware and finish are entered, and a second person has checked them.
  4. Ordered. Purchase order sent, with the supplier's promised date recorded.
  5. Received and inspected. Glass is in your shop, checked against the ticket for size, thickness, cutouts and damage.
  6. Install scheduled. Only now does the customer get a confirmed date.
  7. Installed / punch list. Photos, sign-off and final payment.

The rule that saves the most money is simple: no one can move a job to "Install scheduled" until it's in "Received and inspected." A good tool enforces that. A whiteboard relies on memory.

Build a measure checklist that catches mistakes

The measure visit is your cheapest chance to prevent a remake. Whatever tool you use, make the estimator complete the same fields every time:

  • Width and height at the top, middle and bottom, plus the out-of-square note.
  • Whether finished surfaces (tile, trim, drywall) are in place yet.
  • Glass type, thickness, tint or coating, and edge work.
  • Hardware, hinge locations and cutouts, with a photo of the existing layout if there is one.
  • Access: stairs, tight hallways, elevator, parking and how many people it takes to carry the piece.
  • Location notes: is this glass near a door, a tub, a shower or stairs?

That last item matters because codes treat certain spots as hazardous. Model codes such as the IRC list these locations: the code lists hazardous locations where glazing must be safety-rated, either tempered or laminated (overview). Those commonly include doors, glass beside doors, tub and shower enclosures, glass near stairs and low large panes. A classic miss is a fixed window beside a patio door or a low picture window at a landing.

Also note that codes generally require approved safety glazing, not tempered glass specifically (source), so laminated may be an option. Local amendments vary, so confirm with your building official when a job is borderline. Have the estimator mark "safety glazing required: yes / no / confirm" on every ticket so the order reflects it. Schedule install around the glass, not the other way around

Once statuses are real, the calendar rules get easy.

  • Install windows are built from received dates. If the supplier says Thursday, you hold a tentative slot for the following week. You don't promise anything until the glass is on your rack.
  • Match crew and equipment to the ticket. A large insulated unit or a frameless shower panel needs different hands and gear than a patio door lite. Put "crew size" and "equipment" fields on the job.
  • Batch by geography. When you have several installs in one town, schedule them in the same route block and bundle nearby measures into the same day.
  • Reserve emergency capacity. Storefront break-ins and failed seals don't wait. Keep a few open slots a week, or you'll tear up planned installs every time. See emergency glass repair leads after hours.
  • Buffer commercial jobs. Property managers and general contractors often need access windows, lift rentals or after-hours entry. Capture those on the ticket. Our post on commercial glazing leads covers that side.

Keep the customer in the loop at each status

Most second trips that aren't your fault happen because the customer wasn't ready: the vanity wasn't moved, no one was home or the opening wasn't finished. Automatic messages at status changes solve most of that:

  • Measure confirmed (with what to clear before you arrive).
  • Order placed, with an honest estimated window.
  • Glass received, with a link to pick an install time.
  • Day-before reminder with arrival window and a "reply to reschedule" option.
  • Install complete with a request for a review.

If those go by text, remember the compliance side. As of September 2026, businesses that send automated texts from a standard 10-digit number must register their brand and messaging campaigns with The Campaign Registry (source). You also need to be able to prove how each recipient consented (source). We walk through the setup in our A2P 10DLC registration guide, and the reminder post shows how to reduce no-shows. Off-the-shelf or custom: questions to ask

Plenty of glass shops get by with a CRM plus a shared calendar. Others need something closer to their actual process. Before you buy or build, ask any vendor:

  1. Can I define my own job statuses, and can I block a status change until a field is filled in?
  2. Does the install appointment pull in the measure photos, notes and hardware details automatically?
  3. Can I track supplier PO numbers and promised dates against each job?
  4. Can I see "ordered but not received" and "received but not scheduled" lists in one view?
  5. Can I set crew size and equipment requirements per job and see conflicts?
  6. Do customer texts and emails fire off status changes, and does the platform handle texting registration?
  7. Can I export my data if I leave?

If the tool answers "no" to the first three, you'll still be managing the hard parts on paper. Our guide to CRM pipeline stages explains how to design stages that work, and what a custom business tool is helps you decide when a standard CRM is enough.

FAQ

Common questions

What should glass company scheduling software do?

It should track each job through measure, order, receiving and install, and only let a crew be booked when the glass is physically in your shop and checked. It should also tie the measure notes and photos to the install ticket so the installer sees what the estimator saw.

Should I book the install before the glass arrives?

You can hold a tentative window, but don't confirm it with the customer until the glass is received and inspected. Fabrication dates slip, and a confirmed date you have to cancel costs you trust and a crew day.

Can a glass company trim tempered glass on site if the size is off?

No. Tempered glass can't be cut, drilled or resized after tempering, so a wrong measurement means reordering. That's why the measure visit needs a checklist and a second set of eyes before anything goes to the fabricator.
