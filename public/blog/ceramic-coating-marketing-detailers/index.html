Ceramic Coating Marketing for Detailers: Win High-Ticket Jobs

October 31, 2026 · Digital Marketing

Ceramic coating is one of the best jobs a detailing shop can sell, and one of the easiest to discount into a bad deal. Most owners who run "10% off coatings this month" ads end up attracting people who shop on price and complain about everything else.

This guide covers how to market ceramic coating so the right customers call, ask for your price, and book it. You can do all of it without hiring anyone.

Why discounting hurts more than it looks

A discount comes straight out of profit, not revenue. Here's a hypothetical. Say a coating package is $1,500 and costs you $400 in product, consumables and tech time. You keep $1,100.

Run 20% off and the price drops to $1,200. You now keep $800. To make the same $1,100 in profit, you need roughly 38% more of those jobs. That's more booked days on a calendar that is already limited by how many cars one shop can prep and cure at once.

Discounts also teach customers to wait for the next sale. The better move is to make the price make sense before anyone asks about it.

Sell the process, not the product

Customers can't judge a coating on the day they buy it. They can judge whether you seem careful. Your marketing should make the work visible:

  • Wash, decontamination and clay steps, with photos
  • Paint correction under inspection lighting, before and after
  • Panel prep and wipe-down before the coating goes on
  • Cure time and what the owner should do for the first week

Package your offers in tiers so people choose a level instead of asking for a deal. Here's a simple structure you can adapt:

Tier What it includes Who it's for
Protect Wash, decon, light prep, coating Newer cars in good paint
Correct + Protect Single-stage correction, coating Daily drivers with swirls
Restore + Protect Multi-stage correction, coating, follow-up inspection Enthusiast, classic and high-value vehicles

Set your own prices and warranty terms. The point is that each tier has a clear reason to cost what it costs.

Build the pages that catch high-intent searches

Someone searching "ceramic coating near me" is closer to buying than someone searching "how to wash a car." Give them a page that answers the questions they have before they call:

  1. A dedicated ceramic coating page, not a line item on a services list. Include your tiers, your process, what's included and what isn't.
  2. Vehicle-specific sections or pages, such as trucks, luxury cars, and classics. The owner of a restored muscle car has different worries than a commuter.
  3. A gallery with real jobs, labeled by vehicle and package. Use your own photos only.
  4. A short quote form that asks for make, model, year, paint condition and photos. It saves you a back-and-forth and filters out the unserious.

If your current site can't do this, the web design page for auto restoration and detailing covers how we approach it. The same ideas apply if you build it yourself. Our post on hiring a web designer for your trade business has questions to ask any vendor.

Tighten your Google Business Profile

For many shops, the Google profile is the first thing a buyer sees. Two rules from Google's business representation guidelines matter here. Use as few categories as possible, and pick ones that are specific but still representative of your main business. Don't pad your business name with "Ceramic Coating Paint Correction" if that isn't your real name. Google asks you to enter the name exactly as it appears on your real-world signage and branding.

On photos, Google recommends original photos and videos that accurately represent your business. That's easy for a detailer. Post finished cars, correction close-ups and your shop bay. Skip stock images of someone else's glossy Porsche.

Our Google Business Profile checklist for contractors walks through the rest of the setup.

Get reviews the right way (no discounts)

Coating buyers read reviews closely because they're spending real money. But this is where detailers who love discounts get into trouble.

As of 2026, Google's Business Profile policy on prohibited content lists offering incentives like payment, discounts, free goods or services in exchange for posting a review as not allowed. The same policy says merchants shouldn't discourage negative reviews or selectively solicit positive ones. Google may place restrictions on a profile in addition to removing the reviews.

Federal rules point the same direction. The FTC's final rule on reviews and testimonials prohibits selling or buying fake reviews and lets the agency seek civil penalties against knowing violators. The rule also covers buying positive or negative reviews.

What works instead:

  • Text or email the review link a day or two after pickup, to every customer
  • Ask while the car is still looking its best, but don't make them do it in your shop
  • Reply to every review, including the bad ones
  • Use real review text on your coating page, with the customer's permission

For more on this, see turning Google reviews into marketing.

Be careful with warranty and durability claims

"Lifetime protection" gets attention. It can also cause trouble. In the FTC's Guides for the Advertising of Warranties and Guarantees, the Commission may bring an action against any advertiser who misrepresents the terms or conditions of the warranty offered. The guides include a section on "lifetime" and similar representations.

For written warranties on consumer products, the FTC's Businessperson's Guide to Federal Warranty Law explains that written warranties on consumer products costing more than $15 must be available to consumers before they buy.

Whether those rules apply to your service work is a legal question, and I'm not a lawyer. Here are safe habits regardless:

  1. Put your warranty terms in writing, in plain English, and post them on your coating page.
  2. State what voids it (no maintenance, wrong wash products, new damage).
  3. If a manufacturer's warranty is part of your offer, say whose warranty it is.
  4. Don't say "permanent," "never wash again" or "scratch-proof" unless you can prove it.
  5. Have an attorney review the final language.

Follow up instead of discounting

Most coating leads don't book on the first call. They want to think about it, compare shops, or wait for a payday. A price cut is the lazy reply. A better one is a short follow-up sequence:

  • Same day: Quote with the tier they asked about, plus photos of a similar vehicle you've done
  • Day 2: A text answering the most common worry (cure time, maintenance, how long the car is in the shop)
  • Day 5: An offer of a scheduled inspection or a call to walk through the paint
  • Day 10: A last note with your next open date

Speed counts. Our post on speed to lead for home services explains why, and the estimate follow-up sequence post has a template you can adapt.

If you want to give something extra, give it for booking, not for reviewing. A first maintenance wash included with the coating is a fair example, and it keeps the customer coming back.

Track which leads turn into coatings

Calls and form fills don't tell you much on their own. A shop that gets 40 inquiries a month and books three coatings has a different problem than one that gets 10 and books five. Log each lead's source, the package they asked about and whether they booked. Our guide to tracking where your leads come from covers the setup. A simple spreadsheet works if you don't have a CRM.

After a few months you'll see which channel brings the buyers who pay full price. Put your budget there.

FAQ

Common questions

How do I market ceramic coating without lowering my price?

Sell the prep and the process, not the bottle. Show paint correction work, explain what each package includes, and offer value adds like a scheduled maintenance wash instead of a percent off. Buyers who compare you on process are less likely to compare you on price.

Can I offer a customer a discount for leaving a Google review?

No. As of 2026, Google's policy lists discounts, free goods and free services in exchange for a review as prohibited incentives. Ask every customer for a review, but don't attach a reward to it.

Can I advertise a lifetime ceramic coating warranty?

Only if the written terms back it up and you can show those terms before the sale. The FTC has guides on advertising warranties and on the word "lifetime." Have an attorney review your warranty language before it goes on your website.
