Hardscaping crews get phone calls in March and April from people who want a patio "this spring." By then your calendar is either full of last year's leftovers or empty because nobody followed up in January. Winter pre-booking fixes that by selling, scheduling and sequencing spring work while the ground is still frozen.

This post walks through how to do it: how to count your real capacity, how to structure a winter deposit legally in Massachusetts, which pipeline stages to use, and what to follow up on each week.

## Why winter is the right time to sell spring work

You can't dig in frozen ground, but you can still estimate, design, price and sign. Homeowners also have time to think in winter. They're looking at the backyard from the kitchen window and planning around tax refunds, bonuses or a home equity decision.

The problem is that most hardscaping companies treat winter as dead time. Estimates sit unanswered, past customers don't hear from anyone, and the first real selling starts when the snow melts. That's when every other contractor is also scrambling, and when you're stuck doing estimates instead of running crews.

Say your average install is $18,000 and you can run two crews for 20 working weeks between April and October. If you enter spring with half those weeks already signed, you spend the season finishing work instead of chasing it. That's a hypothetical, but it's the point of pre-booking: fewer open weeks to fill.

## Step 1: Count your spring capacity in crew-weeks

Before you sell a single slot, know how many you have. Don't count jobs. Count crew-weeks, because a paver patio and a stone retaining wall eat very different amounts of time.

| Job type | Typical crew-weeks (your numbers will differ) | What can delay it |
|---|---|---|
| Paver walkway | Short | Base prep, weather |
| Paver patio with seat wall | Medium | Special-order pavers, drainage |
| Retaining wall | Medium to long | Permits, engineering, access |
| Driveway or large paving project | Long | Excavation, drainage, utilities |
| Tree removal or crane work | Short, but tied to equipment | Access, utility lines |

Fill in your own numbers from last season's actual job costing. Then decide how many weeks you'll release for pre-booking (say 60 to 70 percent) and how many you'll hold back for smaller jobs, repairs and callbacks.

## Step 2: Set a winter offer that doesn't break the rules

You need a reason for someone to commit in January instead of May. Good ones are real: a guaranteed start window, first pick of dates, or locking in material pricing. Avoid inventing urgency you can't back up.

Here are the Massachusetts rules to build around, though you should check your own situation with a professional:

- **Written contracts and deposits.** Under the Home Improvement Contractor law, all contracts over $1,000 must be in writing. A deposit paid before work starts cannot exceed one-third of the total contract price, or the actual cost of special-order or custom-made materials that must be ordered in advance. That second part matters if you're ordering specific pavers or natural stone. Read the [Mass.gov contract requirements](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/required-contract-terms-in-a-home-improvement-contract) before you write your winter agreement.
- **Cancellation notice.** Contracts signed at the customer's home generally carry a three-day right to cancel, so build that into your signing process.
- **Start dates.** Winter contracts often give a target window rather than a day. Use language that fits your contract's required start and completion dates, and have your attorney review it.
- **Permits.** Residential retaining walls not over four feet, measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall, are generally exempt from a building permit unless supporting a surcharge. A patio or driveway above the wall counts as a surcharge. See the [Pittsfield building department FAQ](https://www.pittsfieldma.gov/m/faq?cat=17#question-77) for an example, and call the local building department on anything close to the line. Winter is the time to start permit paperwork so it doesn't hold up an April start.
- **Dig Safe.** The law requires notice at least 72 hours before excavation, excluding weekends and legal holidays, but not more than 30 days before. That means a January contract does not mean a January Dig Safe ticket. Put the ticket on your pre-start checklist. The [Massachusetts Dig Safe law](https://www.mass.gov/files/documents/2016/08/nv/digsafe-law.pdf) has the details.

## Step 3: Build pipeline stages for the winter-to-spring handoff

A generic "new lead, quote sent, won, lost" pipeline hides the real problem in winter, which is that nothing is lost. Jobs are just sitting. Add stages that show where each one is stuck. For more on structuring stages, see [CRM pipeline stages for a service business](/blog/crm-pipeline-stages-service-business/).

1. **Inquiry:** a call, form or text came in.
2. **Site visit booked:** winter site visits are fine on bare ground, though snow cover can limit what you see.
3. **Design and estimate in progress:** a layout, material choices, a line-item price.
4. **Estimate sent:** with a date you'll follow up.
5. **Waiting on decision:** the homeowner is thinking, financing or comparing bids.
6. **Signed, deposit received:** the job now holds a slot.
7. **Permit and materials:** permits filed, special orders placed.
8. **Scheduled:** a crew and a start window assigned.

Each stage should have an owner and a next-action date. A job stuck at stage 5 for ten days should trigger a task, not wait for someone to remember it.

## Step 4: Follow up on a schedule, not on a whim

Most winter pre-booking is lost to silence. Someone asked for a quote in November, got a number in December, and nobody called in January. Use the approach in [an estimate follow-up sequence for contractors](/blog/estimate-follow-up-sequence-contractors/), adapted for slow-decision, high-ticket work:

- **Day 2:** confirm they received the estimate and offer to walk through it.
- **Day 7:** send photos of a similar finished project, ideally from your own gallery.
- **Day 14:** mention that spring start windows are filling (only when true) and what happens next if they sign.
- **Day 30:** check whether anything changed, like financing, scope or a second opinion.
- **Monthly after that:** a short note until they say yes or no.

If you text, you need to register first. A2P 10DLC requires brand and campaign registration before you can send messages. Providers differ a lot on turnaround, with some quoting 5–7 business days and others 5-7 weeks, so register in the fall, not the week you want to start. Our [A2P 10DLC guide](/blog/a2p-10dlc-texting-registration-small-business/) covers the steps.

## Step 5: Work the three best winter lead pools

You don't need a huge ad budget to fill spring. Start with people who already know you.

- **Unsold estimates from the last 12 to 18 months.** Some went with someone else, some paused for money. A short, honest message asking if they still want the project often gets replies. The [past-customer text campaign](/blog/reactivate-past-customers-text-campaign/) post has ideas.
- **Past customers.** A patio client may want a fire pit or a walkway next. A tree service client may want stump grinding or a landscaping referral.
- **People searching in winter.** A project gallery with real photos, materials and ranges helps here. See [hardscaping and masonry project gallery websites](/blog/hardscaping-masonry-project-gallery-website/) and, for pricing pages, the [retaining wall pricing page](/blog/retaining-wall-cost-massachusetts-pricing-page/).

Whatever you do, track which pool each signed job came from. [Tracking where your leads come from](/blog/track-where-your-leads-come-from/) is the fastest way to stop guessing next winter.

## Step 6: Keep a waitlist for cancellations and weather

Once a slot is full, don't turn people away. Offer a waitlist. Weather, permit delays and special-order materials will shift your schedule, and someone on the list can fill a gap. Record each waitlisted job with its size, preferred timing and flexibility so you can match it to an open week in minutes.

## Weekly numbers to review

Pick five numbers and look at them every Monday through February:

- Open estimates and their total dollar value
- Signed jobs and deposits received
- Crew-weeks sold versus crew-weeks available
- Estimates with no next action scheduled
- Permits and special orders still outstanding

If crew-weeks sold is lagging by early February, you still have time to run a past-customer campaign or follow up harder on stage 5.
