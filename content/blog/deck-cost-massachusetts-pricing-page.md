Homeowners search "how much does a deck cost in Massachusetts" before they call anyone. If your site gives them no answer, they'll get one from a competitor, a national cost calculator or a neighbor who built a deck in 2019. A good pricing page lets you control that conversation and screens out people who were never going to hire you.

## What the searcher actually wants

Someone typing that phrase usually isn't ready to sign. They want to know if a deck is a $5,000 idea or a $50,000 idea, so they can decide whether to keep going.

That matters for how you build the page. You aren't trying to quote the job. You're trying to give a realistic range, explain what moves it, and ask the people who still want to proceed for the details you need.

The best leads are the ones who read your numbers and still reach out. They've already done the "is this in my budget" math.

## Why showing a number pre-qualifies leads

Hiding all pricing feels safe, but it has a cost. You spend site visits and phone time explaining that a 16x20 composite deck with a railing is not a weekend project at a bargain price.

A page with honest starting points does three things:

- It filters out budget-mismatched leads before they fill out your form.
- It makes the leads you do get easier to quote, because they've read what affects price.
- It builds trust, because you're the contractor who answered the question.

You don't need to publish exact prices. A range tied to clear assumptions works fine.

## What to put on the page

Build the page around the questions you answer on every first call.

1. **A starting price for each project type.** Say "ground-level pressure-treated decks start at $X" or "composite decks with railing typically start at $Y." Use your own real numbers.
2. **A sample project with a price.** Describe one finished job: size, material, height, railing, stairs and the final price range. Use your own completed work, with the owner's permission.
3. **A list of what changes the price.** See the table below.
4. **What's included and what isn't.** Say whether permit drawings, permit fees, demolition of an old deck, and haul-away are in the price.
5. **How payment works.** Show your deposit and payment schedule in plain language.
6. **A short estimate request form.** Ask for the details that make a ballpark quote possible.
7. **A clear service area.** Link to your town pages so homeowners outside your range don't waste your time.

## Explain what drives the price

Most homeowners think of a deck as square footage times a rate. Your crew knows the real drivers are more varied. Put them in a table so the homeowner can see why two decks of the same size can differ a lot.

| Price driver | Why it changes the cost |
| --- | --- |
| Height off the ground | Taller decks need longer posts, more bracing, bigger stairs and guardrails |
| Decking material | Pressure-treated, cedar, composite and PVC differ in material cost and labor |
| Railing type | Wood, aluminum, cable and glass vary widely in cost and install time |
| Stairs and landings | Each stair run adds framing, stringers, treads and railings |
| Footings and site conditions | Ledges, ledge rock, slopes, tight access and poor soil add time |
| Attached vs. freestanding | Ledger attachment adds flashing and inspection detail |
| Extras | Lighting, built-in benches, pergolas, skirting and under-deck drainage |
| Permit and drawings | Plans, fees and inspections take time even when the building is simple |

Keep the explanations short. One or two sentences per row is enough.

## Include the Massachusetts details homeowners don't expect

Local rules are a good reason for a homeowner to pick a pro, so put them on the page. As of 2026, here's what to check and mention.

**Permits.** According to one contractor guide summarizing 780 CMR, a deck attached to the house, more than 30 inches off the ground, or larger than 200 square feet needs a permit. Your local building department makes the final call, and zoning setbacks can apply even to decks that skip the building permit. Say whether you include permit drawings and handle the application. We cover this in more detail in our [Massachusetts deck permit website guide](/blog/deck-permit-massachusetts-website-guide/).

**Licensing.** Contractor guides note that the licensed supervisor's name and license number must appear on the permit application. Mention your Construction Supervisor License and Home Improvement Contractor registration on the page. Check the current requirements for your own work with the state before you publish. The [Mass.gov construction supervisor licensing page](https://www.mass.gov/construction-supervisor-licensing) is a good starting point.

**Contracts and deposits.** Under the state's home improvement law, all contracts over $1,000 must be in writing. The [Mass.gov sample contract guidance](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/home-improvement-contract-requirements-details-and-sample-language) also says a deposit can't exceed one-third of the total contract price, or the actual cost of special-order or custom-made materials. Say your payment schedule plainly. On a hypothetical $24,000 deck, one-third is $8,000, so a homeowner who sees "deposit when we sign, then payments by stage" knows what to expect.

Rules change, so have someone who knows your business confirm the details before you copy any of this onto your site.

## A simple layout that works

Here's a structure you can build on a single page.

1. A headline that uses the search phrase, such as "How much does a deck cost in Massachusetts?"
2. Two short paragraphs with your honest answer: a starting range, and "it depends on these things."
3. Three or four project tiers, each with a starting price, a typical size, a materials list and a photo from your own jobs.
4. The price-driver table.
5. A "what's included" list, including permits and cleanup.
6. Your payment and deposit schedule.
7. A short FAQ.
8. A form with a call button next to it.

## Ask for details that make quoting easy

A short form pre-qualifies leads too. These fields are worth asking for:

- Town and address (so you know if it's in your area)
- Approximate deck size, or "not sure"
- Height off the ground
- Decking material preference
- Rough budget range, as a dropdown with a "not sure yet" option
- Photos of the yard and existing deck
- Timeline: this season, next season, just planning

Keep it to the fields you really use. Long forms lose people, but a few well-chosen questions help you decide who gets a site visit.

## Common mistakes

- **Giving one number with no context.** "Decks start at $15,000" with no assumptions will confuse everyone.
- **Using national averages.** Homeowners in Massachusetts know the market is different here. Use your own jobs.
- **Skipping permits.** If you quote without permit costs and a competitor includes them, you look more expensive for no reason.
- **Burying the form.** Put the call button and form near the pricing, not three pages away.
- **Never updating it.** Lumber, composite and labor costs move. Review the page at least once a year.
