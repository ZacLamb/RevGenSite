A restoration owner who hasn't heard from you in two weeks assumes the worst. A shop owner who answers every "how's it going?" call loses hours of bench time. The fix is a simple, repeatable system for car restoration project updates that answers the owner's questions before they ask.

## Why restoration owners call so much

A restoration isn't an oil change. It can run for months or years, cost more than many houses' kitchens, and involve a car the owner loves. Most of that time, the work is invisible to them: panels at the media blaster, a body on a rotisserie, wiring going in behind a dash.

People call for three reasons:

- **Silence.** No news feels like no progress.
- **Money.** They want to know what's been spent and what's coming.
- **Decisions.** They're waiting on a question you asked and lost track of, or they have one they're afraid to bring up.

Say you have 12 cars on the floor, and each owner calls twice a week for about 10 minutes. That's 12 × 2 × 10 = 240 minutes, or four hours a week of phone time. This is hypothetical, but check your own number. Most of those calls can be answered by a message you send first.

## Build updates around milestones

Calendar-based updates ("every Friday") break down the first busy week. Milestone-based updates don't, because the work itself triggers them.

| Stage | What the owner should see | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Intake and teardown | Photos of the car as received, bagged-and-tagged parts, a list of surprises | Teardown complete |
| Metalwork and body | Before/after photos of rust repair, panel fitment, any scope changes | Each major section done |
| Paint | Primer, color sample, and finished paint photos | Before color is sprayed, and after |
| Mechanical | Engine, driveline and suspension progress, parts ordered and backordered | Parts arrive, build complete |
| Interior and trim | Upholstery samples, gauge and trim choices | Decisions needed |
| Assembly and shakedown | Fit and finish, first start, test drive notes | Car running |
| Delivery | Final walkaround, documents, care instructions | Ready for pickup |

Add a short weekly note during long stretches of quiet work. Two lines and one photo is enough.

## What a good update contains

Most shops send a photo with "making progress!" That generates a phone call. A useful update has five parts:

1. **One clear photo or short video.** Shoot the same angles each time so progress is obvious.
2. **One sentence on what was done.**
3. **One sentence on what's next and roughly when.**
4. **Any decision needed**, stated as a question with a deadline and what happens if it slips.
5. **Cost impact**, even if it's "no change to the estimate."

Keep a short list of standard shots: driver-side front quarter, passenger-side rear quarter, engine bay, underside on the lift, and any problem area close up. Whoever's holding the phone doesn't have to think about it.

## Approvals and change orders: get it in writing

Restorations are where scope creep lives. You open up a quarter panel and find the previous owner's filler and a rotted inner structure. The price changes, and the owner has to approve it before you go ahead.

On the legal side, here's what I confirmed as of 2026. Massachusetts Attorney General regulations at 940 CMR 5.05 govern repair shops. According to the state's consumer guide, they apply to all auto repair shops, auto body shops, and retail stores offering automotive services. Before work begins, a shop must give a written estimate listing the specific repairs, and the customer must sign it. Secondary summaries say the shop must notify the customer and get authorization once the price will exceed the authorized amount by more than $10. Auto body businesses that do damage repair for compensation also need a Motor Vehicle Repair Registration from the Division of Standards.

Sources: the [Massachusetts consumer guide on auto repair](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/massachusetts-consumer-guide-mechanics-of-auto-repair) and a [state-by-state overage summary](https://garageauto.app/can-a-mechanic-charge-more-than-the-estimate/). The [regulation text on Justia](https://regulations.justia.com/states/massachusetts/940-cmr/title-940-cmr-5-00/section-5-05) may not be the latest version. I'm not a lawyer, and how these rules apply to a long-running restoration contract is a question for your attorney. The practical takeaway holds regardless:

- Log every approval with the date, the dollar amount, what changed and how the owner approved it (signature, reply, or e-sign).
- Attach the photos that show why the change was needed.
- Never start extra work on a "sounds good" voicemail.

## Texting owners: consent and registration

Text is where owners will actually read your updates. A few rules apply:

- **Registration.** As of September 2026, A2P 10DLC is the carrier-mandated system requiring businesses that send automated texts from a standard 10-digit number to register their brand and campaigns with The Campaign Registry. It's a carrier-industry requirement rather than a statute, but unregistered messages get filtered before they reach phones. If you text through a CRM or business phone system, check that registration is complete. Our [A2P 10DLC guide for small businesses](/blog/a2p-10dlc-texting-registration-small-business/) walks through it. See also [this explainer](https://www.phoneservicenow.com/resources/weekly-news/a2p-10dlc-business-texting-compliance-2026) and [this one on whether it's required](https://tryready.com/blog/is-10dlc-required).
- **Consent.** Texting is also covered by the federal TCPA, which means you need the customer's permission before texting. Put a consent checkbox or line in your intake paperwork.
- **Opt-out.** Recipients should be able to reply with HELP or STOP. See [this compliance guide](https://www.infobip.com/blog/what-is-a2p-10dlc).

Some owners prefer email or a phone call. Ask at intake which they want, and write it on the job sheet.

## Four ways to run updates, from simple to custom

You don't need software to start. Here's the ladder:

1. **Shared photo album per car.** Free and fast, but it has no structure and no record of approvals.
2. **Spreadsheet plus texts.** Better for tracking stages and hours, but someone has to copy details into messages by hand.
3. **A CRM with pipeline stages.** Moves each job through stages like teardown, body, paint and assembly, and sends a message when the stage changes. See our [CRM pipeline stages guide](/blog/crm-pipeline-stages-service-business/).
4. **A custom status page.** Each car gets a private page showing its current stage, photo timeline, pending approvals, and invoices, so owners can check themselves. This is the kind of thing covered in [what a custom business tool is](/blog/what-is-a-custom-business-tool/).

Pick the lowest rung that solves your actual problem. If photos and an approval log are enough, don't build anything. If you're re-keying the same details into four places, or owners keep asking for the same information, a custom tool starts to make sense.

## Set it up this week

1. **List your stages.** Six to eight milestones that match how your shop works.
2. **Write the standard shot list** and tape it up by the lift.
3. **Pick one owner of updates.** One person sends them, so they happen even when the crew is slammed.
4. **Create three message templates:** milestone reached, decision needed, and delay or parts backorder.
5. **Add a decisions log** with date, amount, and how it was approved.
6. **Set expectations at intake.** Tell owners when they'll hear from you and how, and ask them to hold non-urgent questions for the weekly note.
7. **Review monthly.** Count calls about status. If they aren't dropping, your updates aren't answering the right questions.
