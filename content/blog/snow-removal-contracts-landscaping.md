Snow work can fill the slow months for a landscaping company, but only if the accounts are sold on clear terms, renewed on time and tracked storm by storm. Most of the headaches come from loose contracts and scattered paperwork, not from the snow.

This guide covers how to choose a contract structure, what to put in it, how to sell and renew accounts, and how to keep records that protect you when a customer disputes a bill or a claim.

## Pick the structure before you pitch

Most snow contracts are some version of three structures. Know which risk each one puts on you.

| Structure | How you bill | Who carries a bad winter | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per push (per visit) | A set price each time you clear the site | The customer | Small lots, customers who want flexibility |
| Per event | A flat price per storm | Shared, depends on how "event" is defined | Properties with predictable storm response |
| Seasonal (flat fee) | One price, often billed monthly | You | Properties that want a fixed budget |
| Hybrid | Seasonal base plus per-push above a threshold | Shared | Commercial lots and associations |

The Snow & Ice Management Association's glossary calls the first "per service" and the flat version "per season", typically paid in standardized installments. If your customers or their property managers use those terms, match them in your paperwork.

The seasonal trap is simple. Per push bills every pass, so the customer carries a bad winter. A flat seasonal price puts that risk on you, unless the contract caps the events and total accumulation it was priced on. Hybrid contracts combine a seasonal base fee with per-event charges above a defined threshold. That keeps the budget predictable for the customer without leaving you exposed.

Here is a hypothetical. Say you price a lot at $150 per push and build a seasonal quote on 8 events, so $1,200. If your cost per push is $100 and the winter brings 14 events, you spent $1,400 against $1,200 of revenue. With a cap at 10 events and per-push billing after that, the last four storms bring in $600 instead of nothing.

## What goes in the contract

Write these down for every account, residential or commercial:

- **Trigger depth.** Snowfall amount that starts service, and where it's measured (a named weather station or your own measurement). Say what counts for ice and mixed precipitation.
- **Event definition.** Does one long storm with a lull count as one push or two?
- **Response expectations.** Target start and finish windows, and what you do if a storm runs past them.
- **Scope map.** Lots, walks, steps, loading docks, and where snow gets piled. Note anything you won't do, such as roof snow.
- **De-icing.** Included in the seasonal fee or billed per application.
- **Caps and overages.** Event count, total inches, and what happens above them.
- **Pre-existing conditions.** Cracked pavement, heaved pavers, unmarked curbs and sprinkler heads, documented with photos before the first storm.
- **Insurance.** Your limits, certificate of insurance and additional-insured terms.
- **Payment and renewal.** Billing schedule, late fees, term length and how renewal works.

Trigger depth is a bigger deal than it looks. If the trigger is 4 inches and only 3 fall, you aren't paid to plow, but you could still be held liable if someone is hurt on a lot you're responsible for. Agree in writing on who decides when a lot needs attention below the trigger, and how that instruction is given.

## The liability piece (Massachusetts)

This is general information, not legal advice. Have an attorney review your contract template.

In *Papadopoulos v. Target Corp.* (2010), Massachusetts' highest court discarded the old distinction between natural and unnatural accumulations of snow and ice. The [decision](https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/2010/457-mass-368-1.html) applies normal reasonable-care rules. A snow contractor can be liable for failing to fix hazards on the property it was hired to clear, but hiring a contractor doesn't relieve the owner of liability. Expect both of you to be named in a claim.

That's why indemnity language matters. As of 2026, the law is moving. S.1370, the snow removal service liability limitation bill, was reported favorably by committee in August 2025 and sent to Senate Ways and Means. A provision based on it passed the Senate on July 24, 2026, as part of an economic development bill. It would prohibit indemnification and hold-harmless provisions that make one party assume liability for the other party's negligence. I couldn't confirm that it has become law, so check the [Massachusetts Legislature's bill page](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/S1370) and ask your attorney before relying on it.

Two practical points no matter how that turns out:

- **Insurance is tight.** Insurance for snow contractors has become harder to place, per a [Massachusetts insurance agency's guidance](https://titaninsured.com/blog/snow-and-ice-liability-in-massachusetts-what-business-owners-and-landlords-should-do-before-the-first-storm/). Talk to your broker before you bid, not after you sign.
- **Records are your defense.** Log arrival time, finish time, de-icing applied, weather conditions and who did the work, with photos. New Hampshire passed a law in 2013 exempting contractors from slip-and-fall liability if they keep good records of their snow removal practices. Massachusetts has no such law as of this writing, but the habit helps anywhere.

## Sell it: from walkthrough to signed

Most landscapers already have the leads: mowing, cleanup and hardscape customers who have never been asked about winter. Treat snow as its own pipeline instead of a line in a spreadsheet. Our [pipeline stages guide](/blog/crm-pipeline-stages-service-business/) covers the general idea. A snow version looks like this:

1. **Inquiry or existing-customer flag.** Log the property type, lot size and any current provider.
2. **Site walkthrough.** Measure, photograph pre-existing damage and mark pile areas.
3. **Proposal sent.** Offer two options, such as per push and seasonal with a cap, so the customer picks a structure rather than whether to buy.
4. **Follow-up.** A [simple estimate follow-up sequence](/blog/estimate-follow-up-sequence-contractors/) works here. Most snow proposals die from silence, not price.
5. **Signed and onboarded.** Collect the contract, certificate of insurance, contacts, gate codes and billing details before the first flake.

If you text customers about snow, read our piece on [A2P 10DLC texting registration](/blog/a2p-10dlc-texting-registration-small-business/) first, since unregistered business texting can get filtered or blocked.

## Renew it: the calendar that protects your capacity

Renewals are where recurring revenue gets made or lost. Your capacity (plow trucks, subcontractors, salt supply) depends on knowing your account list early.

- **August–September:** Send renewal offers with next season's pricing and any changes to scope or caps. This fits alongside the [landscaping seasonal marketing calendar](/blog/landscaping-seasonal-marketing-calendar/).
- **Two weeks later:** Follow up by phone or text with anyone who hasn't responded.
- **October:** Final notice with a deadline. Say clearly that unsigned accounts go on a per-push waitlist.
- **After the first storm:** Quick check-in with new accounts to confirm the service matched expectations.

If you're reading this in November with accounts still unsigned, don't wait for the first forecast. Call them this week, then use your [past customer text campaign](/blog/reactivate-past-customers-text-campaign/) for anyone who went quiet.

## Manage it during the season

A snow account needs more data than a lawn account, and it changes every storm. For each property, keep:

- Contract type, trigger depth, caps and renewal date
- Site map, pile areas, hazards and photos
- Contact person and after-hours number
- Storm log: date, start and finish times, de-icing applied, crew, notes
- Billing status, including per-push overages

That's a lot to run from texts and a shared spreadsheet. A CRM can hold the account record, trigger renewal reminders, and send "we're on the way" or "finished" messages. If a missed call during a storm means a customer calls a competitor, set up a [missed call text back](/blog/missed-call-text-back-setup/).
