Commercial glazing work is won differently than residential window replacement. The buyer is often a property manager with a broken storefront at 7 a.m., a general contractor building a bid list, or a facilities person who wants one vendor they never have to think about again. Here is how to get in front of each of them and stay there.

## Who buys commercial glazing, and how they look for you

Different buyers use different channels, so one "get more leads" plan won't work for all of them.

| Buyer | What they need | Where they look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Property manager | Fast emergency board-up, repeat service, clean paperwork | Google, referrals from other managers, existing vendor lists |
| General contractor | A sub with a price, a schedule and the right credentials | Past subs, bid invitations, word of mouth |
| Retail tenant or franchise owner | A broken door or window fixed today | Google Maps and "near me" searches |
| Building owner or landlord | Storefront replacement, tenant fit-outs | Referrals from property managers and architects |
| Public awarding authority | A qualified, certified bidder | Formal public bid process |

Say a property manager oversees 15 buildings and each one averages $1,800 a year in glass repair, door hardware and service calls. That is $27,000 a year from one relationship, and it usually goes to whoever answers first and sends the cleanest invoice. Residential leads rarely behave like that.

## Make it easy for property managers to say yes

Property managers rarely switch vendors on a whim. They add a second or third glazier to their list because the first one was busy or slow. Your job is to be the one they can add in five minutes.

Build a vendor packet and keep it in one folder, ready to send:

1. Certificate of insurance with the limits managers typically ask for
2. W-9
3. Your Massachusetts license and registration details, where they apply to your work
4. A one-page safety summary
5. Three or four project references with building type and scope
6. After-hours phone number and who answers it

Then put a short version of this on your website on a page written for property managers. State what you handle (storefront systems, entrance doors, interior partitions, emergency board-up), your coverage area, and how to reach you after hours. Owners who skip this page lose the call to a competitor who has one.

Emergency response is where many of these relationships start. If you take after-hours calls, read [how to capture emergency glass repair leads after hours](/blog/emergency-glass-repair-leads-after-hours/) and set up a [missed call text-back](/blog/missed-call-text-back-setup/) so no call goes cold.

## Build a commercial section on your website

A residential-style site with a "Request a quote" button and photos of replacement windows won't convince a facilities director. Commercial buyers want proof you've done work like theirs.

Create separate pages for the work you want more of:

- **Storefront glass and entrance doors**, including repair and replacement
- **Emergency board-up and glass replacement**
- **Interior glass partitions and office fronts**
- **Tenant improvement and fit-out glazing**

On each page, show real projects. For each one, list the building type, scope, timeline, and any constraints such as working around business hours or phased installs. The same idea applies in other trades, as covered in [project gallery websites for hardscaping and masonry](/blog/hardscaping-masonry-project-gallery-website/). For glazing, photos of finished storefronts with a short description do more than a generic services list.

Add a location page for each town where you want commercial calls, with real content about that area. For the reasoning, see [why trade businesses need local service pages](/blog/why-trade-businesses-need-local-service-pages/). ## Set up Google Business Profile for a commercial glazier

Many storefront owners search Google Maps, so your profile matters. Two settings trip up glazing companies:

- **Address.** As of 2026, Google's guidelines say a service-area business should hide its address from customers. If you run a shop where walk-in customers come during posted hours, keep the address visible. If you work from a yard or office customers don't visit, hide it.
- **Service area.** Google says the boundaries of your service area shouldn't extend farther than about 2 hours of driving time from your base, and one 2026 guide notes a limit of 20 service areas. List the towns where you'd actually accept a job tomorrow.

Pick categories that describe the commercial work you do, add photos of completed storefronts, and keep hours accurate. Our [Google Business Profile checklist for contractors](/blog/google-business-profile-checklist-contractors/) walks through the rest. Google's own [business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) are the source of record if anything changes.

## Paid search: Local Services Ads or regular Google Ads

Check eligibility before you plan around Local Services Ads. As of 2026, Google offers them only for select service categories, and the glass-related categories that show up in guides tend to be auto glass and window repair. Google is also gradually moving LSA campaign management into Google Ads through 2027, so the setup screens may look different by the time you read this. Google's [LSA overview](https://business.google.com/us/resources/articles/build-online-presence-with-local-services-ads/) is the place to confirm availability for your area.

If commercial glass isn't a category you can select, regular Google Search ads are the practical option. Target searches like "storefront glass repair" and "commercial glass company," add your real service area, and send each ad to the matching page on your site rather than your home page. Keep emergency terms in their own campaign with ads limited to the hours you can answer. For the tradeoffs, read [Google Local Services Ads vs. Google Ads](/blog/google-local-services-ads-vs-google-ads/).

## Winning GC and public bids in Massachusetts

For general contractors, the lead source is mostly relationships plus speed. Make a one-page capability sheet, reply to bid invitations quickly even when you're declining, and send a clear, itemized proposal with exclusions spelled out. Follow up the way you would on any estimate, using a sequence like the one in [our estimate follow-up guide](/blog/estimate-follow-up-sequence-contractors/).

Public work adds paperwork. In Massachusetts, contractor certification is required when a public agency bids a vertical construction project under M.G.L. Chapter 149 or 149A with an estimated cost of at least $150,000. DCAMM lists Glass and Glazing as a standard category of work, covering installation of glass in prepared openings, and the description excludes correlated window systems. Read the category definitions carefully to see which one fits your scope.

Filed sub-bids are separate: areas of work valued over $25,000 must be bid separately as filed sub-bids. Prevailing wage schedules are also part of the bid documents on these projects. Public bidding rules and thresholds change, so confirm current requirements on the [DCAMM certification page](https://www.mass.gov/dcamm-contractor-certification) and in each project's bid documents.

## Track where commercial leads come from

Commercial leads are fewer and worth more, so you can track each one by hand. Log the source for every inquiry: Google Maps, search ad, property manager referral, GC invite, repeat customer. After a quarter, you'll know whether to spend more on search ads or on calling property managers. [Tracking where your leads come from](/blog/track-where-your-leads-come-from/) covers a simple way to do it. Measure how fast you respond too, since [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead-home-services/) matters most when the glass is already broken.

3. Week 3: publish one commercial page with two real project write-ups.
4. Week 4: check Local Services Ads eligibility and, if needed, launch a small Google Search campaign for storefront repair.
